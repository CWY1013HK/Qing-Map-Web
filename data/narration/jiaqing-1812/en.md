## year
The seventeenth year of the Jiaqing reign — rénshēn in the sexagenary cycle, 1812 by the Western calendar. The colophon on this screen map was cut in the first decade of the eighth lunar month of that year. On one earth, in one year, Europe and America were at war; on this painting, the Qing still drew breadth and tribute under the claim of “one family within and beyond.”

## russia
Look first to the northwest. In June 1812, Napoleon of France led the Grande Armée into Russia. As Moscow burned and winter closed in, the invasion collapsed. Yet that campaign did not stand alone: since 1808 Napoleon had already been mired in the Peninsular War — French occupation of Spain and Portugal, guerrilla resistance, and British armies in the field; Goya’s Third of May in Madrid is one of its cruelties. Ice and fire in the east, mud and reprisal on the peninsula — the same turn of the Napoleonic empire from crest to decline. That European blood-trail is not the Qing’s northern frontier war — yet in world time they belong to the years around Jiaqing’s rénshēn.

## england
Now to Britain at the western edge of the sea routes. That same year Britain and the United States went to war — the War of 1812. Ports were closed, merchant ships seized across the Atlantic. The distant storm had not yet shaken the Forbidden City; but the colophon already boasts of myriad realms arriving by ship and interpreter, tribute in tow — the pageantry of a flourishing age.

## india
Look next to South Asia — India. From the mid-eighteenth century the Mughal Empire’s authority thinned; provincial nawabs stood apart, and into that vacuum stepped the British East India Company. Robert Clive laid the foundation in Bengal: after Plassey the Company’s power hardened, and in 1765 it received the Diwani — the right to collect the revenues of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa — turning a trading factory into a territorial state. For decades afterward Company armies fought Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan of Mysore in the south — the Anglo-Mysore Wars — with rocket fire, camps, and baggage trains that Western brushes recorded in detail. By Jiaqing’s rénshēn year the Mughal court still held its name, while much of India already lay under the Company flag. This plate marks only the two characters for India — far simpler than Company rule — yet set against Chinese Tartary to the northwest, it shows how, in the same age, empire stories at Asia’s two ends were not the same.

## tartary
Turn back into the map’s northwest — what Europeans once called 中屬韃靼利亞 (Chinese Tartary), the outer lands of the Manchu realm. Mongolia, Xinjiang, Shengjing, and the far northeast were dependencies and the dynasty’s own rise-lands, not the Eighteen Provinces of China proper. After Qianlong’s campaigns against the Dzungars and the reconquest of the Muslim cities, the New Frontier{mnc: (Ice Jecen)} already lay inside the unified map. By Jiaqing’s day those outer prefectures appear on the plate, no longer the omissions noted of Huang Qianren’s older 1767 chart.

## canton
Look next to Guangzhou on the Lingnan coast. From the twenty-second year of Qianlong, Western trade was confined to the Guangdong customs — what later ages call the Canton System, or “one port of trade.” By Jiaqing’s rénshēn year that regime had already stood for more than half a century. Along the Pearl River the Thirteen Factories lined the shore; French, American, British, and Dutch flags faced one another; merchant craft crowded the stream. Yet look closely at Guangzhou on this map: no factories, no foreign flags, no mark of the monopoly port. Policy had been official for decades; the plate still draws only the provincial city in familiar brushwork. What a flourishing-age atlas chose to proclaim, and what the waterfront actually looked like, were not the same picture.

## tribute
Follow the tribute roads: Korea, Annam, Ryukyu, then the southern and western sea lanes. Tributary relations were not modern borders; they braided ritual, trade, and hierarchy. When the colophon says those who offer tribute and acknowledge the throne are more than the fingers can count, it is folding maritime polities into that same imperial order.

## colophon
So read the Jiaqing-seventeen map as more than scenery. Europe had Napoleon’s ice and fire with Russia; Britain and America fought on the Atlantic; in South Asia the Mughals waned as the East India Company rose; in the east, the engraver was still filling in Jinchuan, Tibet, and Xinjiang, locking dependencies and sea routes into one portable screen — while the lived scene of Canton trade barely appears at all. One year; several worlds; meeting here.
